From photo to netlist

A chip is a few layers of shapes. If you can see the shapes, you can read off the circuit. This page walks through how we do that for the Hitachi HD6845S, a CRT controller of the 1980s, starting from nothing but photographs of its die, and ends with a real piece of its logic running again from the shapes we traced.

Everything below is interactive: drag to pan, scroll to zoom, hover to see what a shape is, click to follow a wire. The 3D views are drawn from the same polygons the tracing project uses, not from a drawing of what they ought to be.

1. What we start with: photographs

A die is the chip itself, a sliver of silicon, once the plastic or ceramic package is taken away. This one was photographed through a microscope in many overlapping tiles that were stitched into a single image, 19,838 by 21,025 pixels. Every position on this page is a pixel of that image, counted from the top-left corner: the tracing project names every feature by its pixel coordinates.

There are five photographs in the project, of two copies of the chip. Some show the chip as it left the factory: the top layer, metal, hides most of what lies beneath. In others the metal was etched away with acid, so the layers below, polysilicon (poly) and diffusion, can be seen. Section 2 says what each layer is for; for now, they are three layers of wiring-and-switch shapes, stacked. We use the photographs together.

Click the picture, then scroll to zoom; drag to pan; double-click to go back to the whole die. The tag marks where the rest of the page goes. The bond pads, where the pins connect, ring the edge; the dense stripes inside are repeated arrays of the same cell.

Zoom all the way in and the neat picture is a landscape of rectangles: the same few shapes repeated, wired together. Reading them is the whole job. The rest of this page is what those shapes mean and how they become a circuit.

2. The technology: a switch you can see

The HD6845S is built in silicon-gate NMOS (n-channel: the current is carried by electrons; the gates are polysilicon), the process of the late 1970s and early 80s. Almost its only component is the MOS transistor, a voltage-controlled switch (a few resistors and capacitors aside). Everything else (gates, latches, counters) is switches wired together.

One transistor

Two islands of n-type silicon (diffusion) sit in a p-type wafer, with a narrow gap between them. The two islands are the transistor's source and drain: current flows from one to the other when the switch is closed. Above the gap, separated by a thin insulating oxide, lies the gate, made of polysilicon (poly). With the gate at low voltage nothing joins the islands, so the switch is open. Raise the gate and its electric field draws electrons into the gap, forming a thin conducting channel that closes the switch.

So a transistor is exactly the place where a poly wire crosses a diffusion wire. That one fact is why the layout is readable from a photograph: find every crossing and you have found every transistor. The width of the crossing (W, across the current) and its length (L, along the current) set how strong the switch is: wide and short is strong, narrow and long is weak.

Layers connect only where something says so. Metal lying over poly is not joined to it unless a contact cut goes down through the oxide between them. Poly and diffusion are joined by a buried contact (or, rarely, by a metal contact that lands on both). Where poly merely crosses diffusion, the result is a transistor, not a connection. With just one layer of metal, wires cross each other by ducking through poly or diffusion under the metal.

The 3D view builds the layers up in the order they sit in the layout, bottom to top. The real manufacturing process is more subtle (for one thing, the source and drain are formed after the gate, aligned to it), but the order of the shapes you see in a photograph is this one.

3. Switches make logic

In this process a logic gate has a fixed shape: a load at the top, pull-down switches at the bottom, and the output node between them.

  • The load is a depletion-mode transistor: one made, by an extra implant, to conduct even with its gate at the same voltage as its source. So it is always a little on. It connects the output to the positive supply (VCC) through a weak path, so the output sits high unless something pulls it down. Its gate is wired to its own output, and in the layout that wire, a tiny buried contact between the load's poly and the output's diffusion, is how a load gives itself away. (The implant itself cannot be seen in the photographs, so the tie is what we go by.)
  • The pull-downs are ordinary switches to ground (VSS). If a pull-down conducts, it overpowers the weak load and drags the output low.

One pull-down is an inverter. Two side by side, either of which can pull the output low, make a NOR gate. Pull-downs in series would make a NAND. Click the buttons to flip the inputs:

That is all the logic there is. A chip like the 6845 is thousands of these, plus clocked switches that let a stored value ride on a gate's capacitance. What makes it hard is purely that the shapes must be read off the photograph without a single mistake.

The hard part is that the picture lies in specific, learnable ways. The colour of a layer changes across the die; metal protects a margin of about 10 pixels beyond its own edge, so a gap between two wires looks like a dark bar that is not a feature; and a dark line across a metal wire is usually the edge of something underneath, not a break in the wire. Our tracing guide records every such trap we have fallen into, more than thirty, because each one cost a wrong connection once.

Why not let software segment it? We tried. In dense logic the edges of poly and diffusion print through the metal as dark lines and cut every wire into fragments; bridging the gaps would also bridge real gaps between neighbouring wires. So the shapes are entered by hand, as polygons in plain-text files, and image processing is kept for checking: comparing repeated cells, measuring colours across a doubtful junction.

7. Checking: how we know it is right

The end product is a circuit exact enough to emulate behaviour no datasheet documents, so a wrong connection is worse than a missing one. Several habits follow from that.

  • Trace what the photos show. Never fill a gap with what a circuit "should" be. If a junction cannot be read, it is marked uncertain with a ? in the trace and written up as an ambiguity, and the work goes on so that it may be settled from another direction.
  • Measure, do not just look. Most wrong readings so far were junctions judged by eye at too low a zoom. A colour profile across the junction in two photographs settles it.
  • Independent review. A second reader, who did not trace the block, checks every junction that decides a connection against the photographs. Only findings that change a connection count against a limit of three rounds; geometry-only fixes are shown harmless by a tool that proves every connection is unchanged.
  • Layout is checked by machine. The extraction counts nets and transistors, flags a contact with nothing under it, a gate that does not fully cross its diffusion, a net with two names.
  • Repeated cells are checked against each other. A cell copied a hundred times must look the same a hundred times, which catches both tracing slips and genuine differences.

Step 11 of the example is one such trap, and a good one to look at again with the net highlighted: a metal wire that lies over four poly wires and connects to none of them.

8. Memory: gates that remember

Chain two gates back to front and the circuit can hold a value. This is the latch at the data bus input of the 6845, as the project recovered it. It is built from the same family of gates as section 5: each half is a load with pull-downs: two in series, in parallel with a third that the other half drives (an AND-OR-invert gate). Each half's output feeds the other half.

Click d and le (latch enable). While le is 1 the pair follows d; when le falls to 0 the pair holds its last value, whatever d does next. The question marks are there because, until le has been 1 once, the circuit's state is not determined.

Glossary

Die
The silicon chip itself, without its package.
Metal, poly, diffusion
The three wiring layers of this process, top to bottom. Metal and poly cross over each other freely; poly over diffusion makes a transistor.
Contact cut / buried contact
The two ways layers are joined: metal to poly or diffusion through a hole in the oxide; poly directly to diffusion.
Source, drain, gate
A transistor's three terminals: the two ends of its channel (source and drain, diffusion) and the control terminal (gate, poly).
Channel, W and L
The strip under the gate where current can flow. W is its width across the current, L its length along it; wide and short conducts more.
Field
The thick oxide over bare silicon between the features; the background of the photographs.
Rail
A wide metal wire that carries VCC or VSS along a row of gates.
Inverter, NOR, latch
A gate whose output is NOT its input; a gate whose output is high only when all inputs are low; a pair of gates that holds a bit.
Depletion load
The always-on weak pull-up transistor of a gate.
Net
A set of shapes that are all electrically one wire.
Pull-down
A switch from a gate's output to VSS.
VCC / VSS
The positive supply and ground.
Trace file
A text file of shapes (rectangles, polygons, wires drawn as a path with a width, contacts), one item per line, in die-photo pixel coordinates, with names on some nets. The extraction tool turns it into a netlist.
Netlist
The list of transistors and which nets their gate and two channel ends touch: the circuit without the geometry.
